“It's not about animals. It's about systems under strain.”

Interview by YSP With SPCA Selangor June 2026 8 min read

SPCA Selangor runs kennels, a subsidised clinic, adoptions, and the Potong Royong population-control model. We spoke to the team about why rescue alone doesn't work, where Malaysia's animal-welfare framework actually breaks, and why the most useful young people in this field are operations thinkers — not just animal lovers.

S

SPCA Selangor

Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, Selangor · Malaysia

An independent animal-welfare society running kennels and catteries, a subsidised clinic, adoptions, and the Potong Royong neutering programme. Note: SPCA Selangor is not affiliated with other organisations sharing the “SPCA” name; the views below are specific to SPCA Selangor only.

This is an edited transcript of an interview YSP conducted with SPCA Selangor. The answers are direct — and, in places, deliberately uncomfortable. We've kept them that way.

Q1

Could you give us a brief overview of SPCA Selangor's day-to-day operations?

Most people think animal welfare is about feeding animals and playing with them. It's not.

Our daily operations revolve around managing the consequences of human decisions — abandonment, irresponsible ownership, and uncontrolled breeding. Yes, we run kennels and catteries, a subsidised clinic, adoptions, and handle enquiries — but the bulk of our effort is spent on surrender cases, which are the most expensive and time-consuming.

Every surrendered animal is not just “rescued.” It becomes a months-long commitment — treatment, vaccination, neutering, recovery, and then hoping for adoption in an already saturated system.

The biggest constraint isn't space or manpower. It's funding — because funding dictates everything else. Without it, even the best intentions collapse. And when we run large-scale neutering programmes, we split our already limited team to manage both long-term prevention and immediate operational pressure at the same time.

That's the reality of animal welfare: it's not about animals. It's about systems under strain.
Q2

Potong Royong is described as a sustainable population-control model. How does it work?

Potong Royong exists because we accepted a hard truth: rescue does not solve the problem.

The programme is built around one principle — if you don't stop breeding, you are just recycling suffering. It started during the flood crisis when multiple parties — SPCA, UPM, MAVMA — came together. What we realised was this: emergency response is temporary, but population control is permanent.

Today, Potong Royong is a coordinated model involving veterinary institutions, local councils, NGOs, and the public.

~700
animals in a major project
~130
animals in a mini project
~3,000
neutered annually

But the real impact is not the numbers. It's behavioural change. When neutering becomes visible, accessible, and normalised:

  • People start doing it
  • Peer pressure works
  • Costs stop being an excuse

This is how you scale impact — not by rescuing more, but by preventing the next generation from being born into the same conditions.

Q3

What are the most persistent misconceptions about animal welfare?

The most damaging belief is this: “Shelters will solve the problem.” They won't.

Shelters are not solutions — they are overflow containers.

Every time someone rescues an animal and sends it to a shelter, they feel like they've helped. But what they've actually done is shift the burden, not solve it.

The second misconception is that animal welfare is about animals. It's not. It's about:

  • Human behaviour
  • Urban planning
  • Policy gaps
  • Cultural realities

If you don't address those, you can build 100 shelters and still fail.

Q4

Where are the biggest gaps in Malaysia's animal-welfare framework?

We don't lack laws. We lack enforcement and alignment.

Malaysia already has a reasonably strong legal framework. The issue is inconsistent enforcement, a lack of coordination between authorities, and — frankly — selective compliance.

Even within the animal-welfare space, there are groups who believe: “Because we are helping animals, the rules don't apply to us.” That mindset is part of the problem.

Real change doesn't come from adding more laws. It comes from:

  • Enforcing existing ones properly
  • Setting clear operational standards
  • Holding everyone accountable — including NGOs
Q5

How can young people contribute meaningfully beyond adoption or donation?

This is where I'll be very direct: adoption is not going to solve the problem. Not at scale.

From our data:

824
enquiries about rescue / surrender
33
enquiries about adoption
3–5%
realistically viable dog adopters

That gap is not emotional — it's structural. A small percentage of people can actually own pets; housing restrictions limit options; financial stability is a factor; and existing owners don't adopt frequently. Cats are higher, but still limited. So unless adoption increases by 700–800%, which is unrealistic, it will never catch up.

What we actually need:

  • Operations thinkers
  • System builders
  • Data-driven planners
  • People who can work with councils and policy

Animal welfare is no longer about “helping animals directly.” It's about reducing suffering at scale through systems.

Q6

What is something you wish received more public attention?

Not all help is helpful.

People want to believe rescue is always good, that saving every animal is the goal, and that shelters are safe solutions. None of these are fully true.

If we keep operating based on emotion alone, shelters will collapse, intake will exceed capacity, and authorities will revert to catch-and-kill.

The goal is not to save every animal. The goal is to reduce overall suffering — sustainably and at scale.

That requires education, higher standards, and the willingness to challenge even our own practices — including within the animal-welfare community itself.
