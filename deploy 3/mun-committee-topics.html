01
General Assembly

UNGA — Third Committee

40 seatsIntermediateOutcome · Resolution

Topic on the placardAddressing the nexus between climate migration and human rights: a framework for binding international protection standards.

Context

By the late 2020s, climate-driven displacement is no longer a future scenario — it is an administrative reality that the existing refugee architecture was never written to handle. The 1951 Refugee Convention does not recognise environmental displacement as grounds for protection, leaving tens of millions in a legal grey zone between "migrant" and "refugee."

The Third Committee must decide whether the international community creates a new binding protection standard, extends existing instruments, or leaves the question to regional and bilateral arrangements. Each path redistributes obligation — and cost — between origin states, transit states, and destination states.

Key questions before the floor

  • i.Should "climate-displaced person" become a protected legal category, and if so, who bears the burden of proof for causation?
  • ii.How are protection obligations distributed between high-emitting states and the frontline states absorbing displacement?
  • iii.What is the role of planned, dignified relocation versus reactive emergency response?
  • iv.Can a binding standard survive without enforcement — and what enforcement is realistic?

Blocs to watch

Frontline & small-island states

Push hardest for binding obligations and dedicated finance; least capacity to absorb cost alone.

High-income destination states

Favour voluntary, capacity-based frameworks; wary of open-ended legal commitments.

Large transit economies

Seek burden-sharing and finance in exchange for hosting; the swing vote of the room.

02
Security Council

UN Security Council

15 seatsAdvancedOutcome · Directives

Topic on the placardRestoring regional stability in the Indo-Pacific: sovereignty, grey-zone tactics, and the limits of multilateral response.

Context

The Indo-Pacific has become the testing ground for "grey-zone" conflict — coercion that stays deliberately below the threshold of open war: maritime militia, infrastructure pressure, disputed exclusive economic zones, and information operations. The Security Council's tools were built for declared aggression, not for ambiguity engineered to fracture consensus.

Delegates must navigate the structural reality of the Council itself: the veto, the divide between permanent and elected members, and the credibility cost of resolutions that cannot be enforced. This is a committee about what the Council can actually do, not what it wishes it could.

Key questions before the floor

  • i.What constitutes a "threat to international peace and security" when no shot has been fired?
  • ii.Can the Council act meaningfully where a permanent member holds a direct stake?
  • iii.What is the line between freedom of navigation and provocation?
  • iv.Should regional mechanisms (ASEAN, Quad) be empowered, or does that erode the Council's authority?

Blocs to watch

Permanent members (P5)

Each guards strategic interests; the veto shapes every draft before it reaches the floor.

Regional elected members

Closest to the consequences; broker language the P5 can live with.

Non-aligned voices

Press for restraint and process; leverage moral authority over hard power.

Difficulty · Advanced — for experienced delegates comfortable with the veto Read the background guide →
03
Ad Hoc Crisis

Joint Cabinet — Crisis

25 seatsExpertOutcome · Live directives

Topic on the placardThe cascading financial contagion of 2026: a G20 Emergency Finance Ministers' Council facing sovereign debt cascade and currency warfare.

Context

A sovereign default in one mid-sized economy has triggered a chain reaction: capital flight, currency collapse, and an emergency that the post-2008 financial firewalls were supposed to prevent. Delegates sit as Finance Ministers and central-bank governors in a G20 emergency session — and the situation will not hold still.

This committee does not debate a static topic. The Crisis Director introduces injections on a published and a hidden schedule, and every directive the cabinet passes feeds the next development. Delegates are graded on the consequences they cause, not the speeches they give.

Opening pressures

  • i.Coordinated liquidity support versus protecting domestic reserves — who blinks first?
  • ii.Capital controls: stabilising or contagion-accelerating?
  • iii.How does the cabinet respond to currency interventions made outside its mandate?
  • iv.What happens when a back-channel deal between two ministers leaks to the floor?

Portfolios & alignment

Surplus economies

Hold the reserves everyone needs; will extract conditions for any rescue package.

Exposed debtor states

Need liquidity now; weigh sovereignty against survival.

Institutional actors

IMF and central-bank seats with tools but contested legitimacy.

See the full crisis-committee mechanics on the main MUN page.

04
Environment

UNEP Special Session

40 seatsIntermediateOutcome · Resolution

Topic on the placardGoverning the geoengineering frontier: solar radiation management, transboundary risk, and the architecture of global atmospheric governance.

Context

As emissions targets slip, solar geoengineering — deliberately reflecting sunlight to cool the planet — has moved from fringe theory to active research. The technology is comparatively cheap, which means a single state, or even a wealthy private actor, could deploy it unilaterally with planetary consequences. There is currently no governance regime for any of it.

UNEP must decide whether the world builds a moratorium, a research framework, or a full governance architecture — and who holds the authority to say "stop." The committee weighs precaution against the moral hazard of doing nothing while the climate window narrows.

Key questions before the floor

  • i.Who has the right to alter the global climate, and who must consent?
  • ii.Should research be paused, governed, or accelerated under safeguards?
  • iii.How is liability assigned for transboundary harm from deployment?
  • iv.Does a governance regime legitimise the technology by regulating it?

Blocs to watch

Research-capable states

Want a permissive framework that keeps the option open under oversight.

Climate-vulnerable states

Split between precaution and demanding a seat at any deployment decision.

Precautionary coalition

Push for a moratorium until governance exists; invoke the precautionary principle.

05
Youth Assembly

YSP Youth Special Assembly

30 seatsBeginner-friendlyOutcome · Policy paper

Topic on the placardRenegotiating the intergenerational social contract: youth representation mechanisms in multilateral climate finance frameworks.

Context

Decisions about climate finance made today bind the generation that will live with their outcomes — yet that generation has almost no formal seat at the table. The Youth Special Assembly asks delegates to design the mechanisms that would actually give young people decision-making power, not just consultation, in how climate finance is raised, allocated, and held accountable.

Built for first-time delegates, this committee trades veto politics for design thinking: the room produces a policy paper proposing concrete representation mechanisms, and the dais coaches procedure as the session runs.

Key questions before the floor

  • i.What does meaningful youth representation look like beyond a symbolic seat?
  • ii.How do you build accountability that outlasts a single conference cycle?
  • iii.Should youth bodies have advisory power, voting power, or budget authority?
  • iv.How is "youth" defined fairly across very different national contexts?

Perspectives in the room

Youth delegations

Argue for binding mechanisms and a real budget line, not consultation theatre.

Established institutions

Favour advisory roles; cite mandate, capacity, and continuity concerns.

Funder states

Want accountability attached to any new representation structure.

Difficulty · Beginner-friendly — designed for first-time delegates Read the background guide →
